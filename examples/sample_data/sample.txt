It was the best of times, it was the worst of times, it was the age of wisdom, it was the age of foolishness, it was the epoch of belief, it was the epoch of incredulity, it was the season of Light, it was the season of Darkness, it was the spring of hope, it was the winter of despair.

We had everything before us, we had nothing before us, we were all going direct to Heaven, we were all going direct the other way. In short, the period was so far like the present period, that some of its noisiest authorities insisted on its being received, for good or for evil, in the superlative degree of comparison only.

There were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a plain face, on the throne of England; there were a king with a large jaw and a queen with a fair face, on the throne of France. In both countries it was clearer than crystal to the lords of the State preserves of loaves and fishes, that things in general were settled for ever.

It was the year of Our Lord one thousand seven hundred and seventy-five. Spiritual revelations were conceded to England at that favoured period, as at this. Mrs. Southcott had recently attained her five-and-twentieth blessed birthday, of whom a prophetic private in the Life Guards had heralded the sublime appearance by announcing that arrangements were made for the swallowing up of London and Westminster.

The mail coach rattled through the streets of the great city with its usual speed, carrying as usual a guard armed to the teeth. Inside sat the passengers; some sleeping, some drowsing, some watching the scenery. The night was dark and cold. The roads were rutted and muddy from the autumn rains. Horses splashed through puddles. Lanterns swung at the corners. The city smelled of smoke and horses and rain.

In the midst of the city there was a court, and in the court there was a small prison. In the small prison there was a man who had been there a very long time, so long that he had forgotten what the outside world looked like. He sat in the darkness and waited, as he had waited for many years, for something he could not name.

The revolution began quietly, as revolutions often do. A pamphlet here, a whisper there, a meeting in a cellar by candlelight. The king knew nothing of it. The queen knew nothing. The nobles in their carriages rolling past the hungry crowds knew nothing, or chose to know nothing, which amounts to the same thing in the end.

There is nothing so powerful as an idea whose time has come. And the time had come. The people had endured. The people had waited. The people would wait no longer. Across the channel, across the ocean, the same idea stirred in hearts that had never heard of France, had never heard of England, had never heard of kings at all.

Liberty is a word that means different things to different people. To some it means the right to do as they please. To others it means freedom from hunger and want. To still others it means the right to speak, to write, to think, to worship as conscience demands. The revolution would discover which meaning it held in its own heart only after the fighting was done.

And so it ends as it begins — in darkness and uncertainty, in hope and despair, in the knowledge that what was done cannot be undone, and what was suffered cannot be forgotten. The wheel turns. The river flows. The dead are buried and the living go on, as they must, as they always have, as they always will.
